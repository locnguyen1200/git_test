A Counterfeit

Intro

Initially, I bought this repainted black Leica M4, with the intention of reselling it. It was ‘advertised’ as CLA’d by a renowned CLA technician in Vietnam, so I pulled the trigger without hesitation; for what it was, it was a steal. However, I soon came to realize why it was ‘cheaper’ than expected.

Adjusted and fitted.

The Leica M4 is a legendary rangefinder in Leica’s lineup. It was the final Leica rangefinder to utilize the manual “adjust and fit” production method, as subsequent models like the M4-2 transitioned to standardized manufacturing with interchangeable parts. Constructed with brass top and bottom plates and brass internal gearing, a departure from the zinc-alloy and steel components seen in later iterations, the M4 possesses a distinct, substantial heft. Its mechanical operation offers a silky, dampened sensation during the film advance, contrasting with the more rigid, tactile feedback characteristic of its successors, here compared to the M6.

A Look Back At History.

As many analogue enthusiasts are already well aware through countless video essays and forum discussions, the story of Leica, including models like the M4, M4-2, M4-P, M5, CL, and CLE, and its historical transition, is widely documented. The production of the M4 was a difficult period for Leitz. With the rising popularity of Japanese SLRs in the 1960s and 1970s, the demand for rangefinders was drastically cut. Leitz attempted to compete with the rising dominance of modern SLRs by introducing the M5 in 1971, which incorporated an internal spot meter and a redesigned shutter assembly. While this resulted in the most silent mechanical operation of any M model, the pursuit of innovation necessitated a larger, more cumbersome body. By deviating from the company’s storied design aesthetic, the M5 ultimately became a commercial disappointment.

This almost pushed Leitz to bankruptcy, forcing it to pivot to more cost-effective manufacturing. To rescue the brand from this financial crisis, the M4-2 and M4-P were introduced as modernized, cost-effective solutions that effectively saved Leitz by shifting production to Midland, Ontario, and embracing standardized manufacturing…and other stories with the CL and CLE, which will be covered when I eventually get my hands on one.

My Leica M4.

So why was my Leica M4 ‘cheaper’ than expected? The slow-speed mechanism had issues. Speeds from 1/8 to 1 second stuttered or completely stopped, so much for CLA’d. The film advance lever and front levers were replaced with an all-brass lever from the M3/M2. Despite these shortcomings, my love for the rangefinder remains steadfast; the film advance still delivers that signature silky, dampened sensation, complemented by a substantial, tactile heft and a crystal-clear viewfinder.
